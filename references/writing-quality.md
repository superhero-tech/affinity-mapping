# Writing observations, themes, and opportunities

Read before extraction and use again before presenting the synthesis. Apply these criteria in any output language; they do not depend on a language-specific editing skill.

## Write directly in the output language

Use the vocabulary, grammar, and sentence structure natural to the intended reader. Do not draft labels as English formulas and translate them word for word. The skill's English examples illustrate meaning, not required wording. Preserve useful domain terms and product names when participants use them; explain unfamiliar terms only when needed.

## Match the wording to the evidence

- **Observation:** say what this participant said, did, experienced, or did not know in a concrete situation. Make the actor and action clear when they would otherwise be ambiguous. Keep one observation per nugget. Avoid abstract noun chains, compressed shorthand, and explanations of motive that the quote does not establish.
- **Theme:** name the behavior or problem shared by its nuggets in a short, understandable statement. A label must make sense on its own and fit its evidence; it must not broaden one participant's experience into a claim about everyone.
- **Opportunity:** express an evidenced need, pain, or desire in words the customer could naturally use. Include enough context to understand the need, without packing the entire evidence trail into the title. First-person wording is a synthesis, not a verbatim participant quote. Keep solutions and business goals out of it.
- **Divergence or summary:** state who differs, in what situation, and what remains unknown. Preserve uncertainty and distinctions between reported experience, observed behavior, and an analyst's inference.

For example, "Experiences temporal ambiguity around renewed engagement" is obscure; "After two weeks, the recruiter does not know whether the candidate is still interested" states the same situation concretely. This is an illustrative example, not evidence to reuse in a project.

## Titles and short previews

Read the title and preview without neighboring cards or linked documents. The reader should be able to picture who needs or does what, using the concrete situation from the quotes. Prefer a natural descriptive sentence to a compressed research label; do not impose a fixed title formula. Preserve those details when shortening for HTML or a handoff. The linked source provides evidence and detail, not the missing basic explanation. This is part of this skill's own quality review, independent of any separate editing skill.

## Review language and fidelity together

Before presenting the Markdown synthesis, read each observation and label as a standalone note:

1. Can the intended reader understand who is doing or needing what, without decoding analyst shorthand?
2. Does it sound natural when read aloud in the output language, with correct grammar and clear references?
3. Does every claim still follow from the linked evidence? Keep qualifications such as "may", "reports", or "in this interview" when needed; do not turn association into causation or a possibility into a fact.
4. Is each opportunity something the customer could plausibly say, and does its scope still match its nuggets?

Revise unclear wording in the Markdown source, then project the same wording into JSON and HTML. Preserve IDs, sources, evidence links, cluster membership, and review status during a wording-only edit. If the edit would change the interpretation or scope, treat it as a synthesis revision requiring review rather than silently polishing it.

Never correct grammar, simplify, or translate the verbatim source quote itself. If a translation is requested, label it separately and retain the original. Keep analysis in the requested output language even when source interviews use another language.
